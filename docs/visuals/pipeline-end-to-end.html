How a Prospect Gets Found

Four public government databases go in. One ranked list of physicians worth calling comes out. Here is every step in between, in plain English.

The idea in one sentence

A physician who just got licensed, then formed their own practice, then bought a house is becoming wealthy — and probably doesn't have a financial advisor yet. The system's whole job is to spot that pattern in public records and put those people at the top of a list.

Step by step

  1. 01

    Sourcing — four databases, and the order matters

    All four are free, public, government-run. No paid data, no scraping, no API keys. But they don't all start at once — the NPI Registry has to finish first, because it hands the other three the IDs they are looked up by. Once it has, those three run side by side. That's what keeps the matching honest.

  1. 02

    One format — four messy feeds become two shapes

    Each source returns completely different JSON. A small translator (an "adapter") per source rewrites every row into one of exactly two shapes:

    • A person record — name, specialty, licence, NPI, practice address, phone. Only the NPI Registry and IL licensing produce these.
    • An event record — something that happened to a person: they own a company, they bought a house, they changed roles. Only Medicare billing and county deeds produce these.

    Everything downstream — matching, signals, scoring — only ever sees those two shapes. That's why adding a fifth data source later doesn't touch any of the scoring logic.

  2. 03

    Same person? — merging duplicates

    The same physician shows up in the NPI Registry and again in the licensing roster. The system decides whether two records are one human:

    • Same licence number → certainly the same person. Perfect evidence, no judgement needed.
    • Same last name, same state, same first name ignoring middle initials → confident match. "John Smith MD" and "John A Smith MD" merge.
    • Anything weaker → they stay two separate people. A missed merge is acceptable; a wrong merge is not.
  3. 04

    One profile — attaching the events

    Now the company records and property records get hung onto the right person. This bar is set higher than the merge bar, because attaching someone else's house to a prospect would silently corrupt their score.

    Medicare records attach by NPI, so there's no name risk at all. Deeds must match exact first name + exact last name + same state. Near-misses are dropped rather than guessed at. Every single merge and attach writes a row recording the score and a human-readable reason — "license number match", "exact first and last name, same state" — so any claim on a dossier can be traced back to the records that produced it.

The rule that shapes everything: gates reject, scoring weighs. Everything up to this point is a yes/no gate about trust — is this a physician, is this the same person, does this deed belong to them. Everything after this point is a sliding scale about value. Once a prospect is through the gates, they are never rejected again — they can only be worth more or less.

  1. 05

    Signals — turning facts into things you can score

    The merged profile is read for six specific signals, each rated from 0 to 1 on how strong it is. No signal means no points — the system never guesses at a missing fact.

The ownership trick, since it's the clever bit

There is no free database of "which doctors own their practice." But before a physician can bill Medicare, they must declare which company gets paid. If that company is named after them — "Beth Adams Medical Services PLLC" — they almost certainly own it. Nobody routes their income through a stranger's company bearing their own name.

The system flags that as an inference, not a fact: it scores slightly lower than a registry record would, and the dossier says "name-matched billing group" rather than claiming proof.

  1. 06

    Score — two questions, blended

    Every prospect is scored on exactly two questions, and nothing else.

Two things are deliberately kept out of the score. Match confidence is shown beside the score, never multiplied into it — the score says how good the prospect is, confidence says how sure we are it's the right person. And there's no AI in the scoring at all: same data in, same score out, every point traceable to a source record.

Why nobody scores 100 on day one

The best possible first-run score is 91. The missing points are honest headroom: proving someone moved requires comparing two months of data, so a single snapshot can never earn those points. A score above 91 means the system watched that person change over time.

  1. 07

    Output — what actually reaches the advisor

    Three things, plus a running history:

    • The ranked board — highest score first, so the top of the list is where to start the day.
    • The dossier — every fact behind the score, each one traceable to the record and the match reason that produced it, with a short plain-English write-up of why this person, why now.
    • The contact kit — practice address, practice phone, the one trigger to lead the conversation with, and the handling rules. Notably: never write to a home address, and never mention the property purchase. It raises urgency internally; it's not something you say out loud.

    Every run appends a new score rather than overwriting the old one, so the board can show who is climbing — which is usually a better reason to call than the score itself.

Two details worth knowing

Re-running is safe

The sweep is idempotent — running it twice doesn't create duplicates. Existing prospects are found by NPI (or name + state) and updated in place, and any field that changed is logged as "was X, now Y" so you can see what moved.

New strangers have to be new

Someone already in the book always gets updated. But an unknown physician is only added if they're a fresh arrival — licensed or enumerated within the last six months. Long-established strangers are skipped, so the book fills with genuinely new entrants instead of decades of backlog.
