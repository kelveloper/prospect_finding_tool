Speaker 1

The problem

0:00 – 1:20
DoOpen on a plain slide or the scoreboard, not yet clicked. Talk to the room, not the screen.

Every advisor in the country is chasing the same people — the ones who are already rich. And by the time somebody shows up on a wealth list, they’ve had an advisor for ten years. You’re not pitching at that point. You’re trying to break up a marriage.

So we asked a different question. Can we find people in the year before they get wealthy?

Take a doctor. They finish training. They get licensed. They open their own practice. They buy a house. Somewhere inside those eighteen months they go from a salary to real money — and most of them still don’t have anybody managing it.

NoteSlow down here. This chain is the whole thesis, and it’s the part a manager will repeat to someone else.

And here’s the part that made us build this. Every one of those steps is public record. The license is public. The practice is public. The deed is public. They were just sitting in four different government databases that nobody had put together.

So we put them together. [Speaker 2] will show you what that looks like.

Speaker 3

How it works underneath

3:20 – 5:00

Underneath, it’s one pipeline. Python and FastAPI. One command runs the whole thing end to end.

DoPut this diagram up and trace it left to right with your cursor while you talk.

We start with the national doctor registry. That gives us who they are, their specialty, and their state license number. We take those license numbers to the Illinois licensing board, which tells us when they were licensed and whether it’s still active. We take their NPI to Medicare, which tells us what company they bill under. And we take their names to Cook County deed records.

The important bit is that each source is queried using the answer from the one before it. We never bulk-download anything. We only ever ask about people we’re already tracking.

Then three things happen. We work out which records belong to the same human. We turn the facts into signals. And we weigh the signals into a score.

DoThese two rules are the answer to most objections. Say them slowly.

Two rules we don’t bend. One: the scoring is completely deterministic — there is no AI anywhere inside it. Same data in, same number out. That’s what makes it defensible. Two: a wrong match is worse than a missed one. If we can’t prove two records are the same person, we throw it away rather than guess.

Sixty automated tests, all passing. Four government APIs, all free. Our data cost today is zero.

Cut firstDrop this if you’re over time — but keep “our data cost today is zero,” managers remember that one.

Where it goes next: more states and more professions — lawyers, dentists, the same pattern. That’s one new connector each; the scoring engine doesn’t change. And we’re already storing every advisor’s good-fit or not-a-fit verdict, so eventually we tune the weights against real outcomes instead of our own guesses.

Happy to take questions.

Whoever knows it best answers — don’t all jump in

Questions you will get

“Is any of this a privacy problem?”

All four sources are public government datasets — the same records anyone can look up. Nothing is scraped, nothing is bought, no personal data is inferred beyond what the records say. Worth adding plainly: this is research output, not an approved outreach list. A human reviews before anyone gets contacted.

“Why is nobody scoring above sixty-something?”

Missing inputs, not missing logic. Two things fill that in: paid business-registry data, which proves practice ownership instead of us inferring it, and career moves, which need a second monthly sync to compare against. Ninety-one is the day-one ceiling by design — anything above that has to be earned by watching someone over time.

“Where’s the AI?”

Deliberately outside the scoring. The score has to survive a compliance conversation, so it stays rules-based. Where AI helps is around it — suggesting matches on ambiguous names, where the rules still make the final call, and writing the plain-English summary an advisor reads.

“How do you know two records are the same person?”

Mostly we don’t have to guess. The license number and the NPI are exact government IDs shared across datasets. Only the property match is name-based, and there we require an exact first and last name in the same state — anything less gets dropped, and there are tests that exist purely to prove near-misses still get rejected.

“Why doctors?”

Because it’s the cleanest public data in the country — a national registry, state licensing boards, and a federal database that names who each doctor bills. It’s the easiest place to prove the idea works. Lawyers and dentists are the same pattern with a different connector.

“What would it take to run this for real?”

Three things: lift the demo cap so we pull whole specialties instead of a sample, buy the business-registry feed, and run it on a nightly schedule so the score history builds up. None of that changes the engine.

Say it this way, not that way

  • It’s a research tool, not a lead list. If someone hears “call list,” you’ll spend the rest of the meeting on compliance.
  • Ownership is inferred from Medicare billing, not read off a filing. Say “we worked it out,” never “we know.” The screen labels it as an inference — let it.
  • Don’t apologize for the scores being in the fifties. Ninety-one is the day-one ceiling, on purpose, and that’s a better story than a system handing out perfect hundreds on its first run.
  • If someone notices nearly every surname starts with “A”: that’s a demo page limit, one parameter. Say it before they find it if you can.
  • These are real named people from public records. Keep the tone factual on screen — no speculating about anyone.
