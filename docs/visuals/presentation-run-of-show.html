Before the room is watching

Pre-flight

  • Start the backend on the real database. .venv/bin/uvicorn app.main:app --port 8000 — the default prospects.db holds the 219 scored physicians. Do not point DATABASE_URL at a file that does not exist; SQLite creates it empty and you will be on stage with a blank board.
  • Start the frontend — cd frontend && npm run dev, then open localhost:3000.
  • Do not run a live ingest on stage. It returns immediately and runs in the background now, but it still calls four government APIs and takes about two minutes; a second start returns 409, and the weekly gate returns 429 unless you pass force=true. Run it beforehand if you want fresh data.
  • Load the screens in tabs ahead of time so no click waits on a spinner: the board, Rahul Aggarwal, Beth Ann Adams, Smita Aggarwal, and a score breakdown.
  • Sanity check — curl localhost:8000/health returns ok, and pytest -q shows 74 passed. Worth having that terminal open if the manager asks.
  • Know your top name. Rahul Aggarwal is rank 1 and carries the whole thesis: Illinois licence two months ago, a $1,100,000 house two months ago. If the board has been re-ingested, check who is on top before you walk in.
  • Agree the handoff line. Each speaker ends by naming the next person. It sounds rehearsed because it is.
Speaker 1

The problem, and what we built

0:00 – 1:20
Do · the problemOpen on the title slide. Talk to the room, not the screen.

Today we’re presenting our Emerging Affluent Prospecting proof of concept.

The problem is that every advisor chases the same people — the ones who are already visibly wealthy. And by the time somebody lands on a wealth list, they’ve had an advisor for ten years. You’re not pitching at that point. You’re trying to break up a marriage.

So we asked: can we find them earlier, while the wealth is still forming?

NoteLand the problem before you mention doctors, data or scores. If the room doesn’t feel “too late,” nothing after this matters.
Do · why physiciansMove to the opportunity slide.

We started with physicians — twenty-three thousand new openings a year, and a career that leaves a public trail: licence, practice, ownership.

Cut firstIf you are running long, drop the openings figure and start at “a career that leaves a public trail.” Nothing later depends on the number.

And we’re looking for change, not status. A new licence is more actionable than twenty years of tenure. So the hypothesis is that recent, corroborated transitions tell us not just who is worth calling, but when.

NoteThat last sentence is the thesis. Pause after it — it is the line a manager repeats to somebody else.
Do · the solutionMove to the engine slide. This is the “so we built it” beat.

And every one of those transitions is public record — sitting in four government databases that nobody had joined up.

So that is what we built. The engine pulls all four, works out which records are genuinely the same person, and ranks them. And it doesn’t just return a number — it tells the advisor why this person matters now, and what to do next.

Two hundred and nineteen Illinois physicians, ranked. [Speaker 2] will show you.

Speaker 3

How it works, and the decision

3:40 – 5:30
DoPut this diagram up and trace it left to right with your cursor while you talk.

Underneath, it’s one pipeline. We start with the national physician registry — who they are, their specialty, and their state licence number. We take those licence numbers to the Illinois licensing board, their NPI to Medicare, and their names to Cook County deeds.

The important part is that the registry goes first, and its identifiers drive targeted queries into the other three. We never bulk-download anything. We only ever ask about physicians we already hold.

Then three things happen. We work out which records belong to the same human. We turn the facts into signals. And we weigh the signals into a score.

DoThese two rules answer most objections. Say them slowly.

Two rules we don’t bend. One: the scoring is completely deterministic — there is no AI anywhere inside it. Same data in, same number out. That is what makes it defensible in a compliance conversation.

Two: a missed signal is safer than attaching another person’s evidence. If we can’t prove two records are the same person, we drop it rather than guess. Attaching somebody else’s house or business to a prospect would quietly corrupt the whole thing.

NotePause after that second rule. It should sound like a design principle, not a technical limitation.

Seventy-four automated tests, all passing. Four government APIs, all free. Our data cost today is zero.

Cut firstDrop the test count if you are over — but keep “our data cost today is zero.” Managers remember that one.
DoWhat production would add. This stops anyone assuming the POC already does everything.

What we’ve shown is a working end-to-end core. Going to production would need broader data agreements and coverage, stronger data-quality and governance controls, authentication and audit, and integration into the advisor’s existing workflow — Coach or the CRM.

And collecting advisor outcomes, so the ranking can eventually be calibrated on what actually leads to engagement instead of on our own guesses. We’re already storing every good-fit and not-a-fit verdict for exactly that.

Do · the closeThe decision slide. This is a conclusion, not more architecture.

So we evaluated the concept across four questions.

Is it desirable? Physicians give us a repeatable population of career and financial transitions where earlier prospecting creates value. Is it feasible? This is a working path from public data through ranking into an advisor-facing experience.

Is it credible? Every recommendation traces back to dated public evidence rather than an unexplained score. And is it expandable? The architecture is built around adapters — the same core extends to other professions, states and sources. That is one new connector each; the scoring engine doesn’t change.

So the opportunity isn’t another list of leads. It’s an engine that tells an advisor who may be worth engaging, why they were surfaced, and why now.

NoteThen stop. That is the ending — don’t add a summary after it. “Happy to take questions” is fine; anything more dilutes it.
Whoever knows it best answers — don’t all jump in

Questions you will get

“Is any of this a privacy problem?”

All four sources are public government datasets — the same records anyone can look up. Nothing is scraped, nothing is bought, no personal data is inferred beyond what the records say. Worth adding plainly: this is research output, not an approved outreach list. A human reviews before anyone is contacted.

“Why sixty-forty?”

Say this on slide 7 before it gets asked. They are transparent prototype weights, set so fit outranks timing — a strong prospect at an average moment still matters more than a weak one at a perfect moment. In production they would be calibrated against advisor outcomes, which is why we already store every good-fit and not-a-fit verdict.

“Why is nobody scoring above sixty-something?”

Missing inputs, not missing logic. Two things fill it in: paid business-registry data, which proves practice ownership instead of us inferring it, and career moves, which need a second monthly sync to compare against. Ninety-one is the day-one ceiling by design — anything above it has to be earned by watching someone over time.

“Where’s the AI?”

Deliberately outside the scoring. The score has to survive a compliance conversation, so it stays rules-based and deterministic — same data in, same number out. Where AI helps is around it: proposing matches on ambiguous names, where the rules still decide, and writing the plain-English summary an advisor reads.

“How do you know two records are the same person?”

Mostly we don’t have to guess. The licence number and the NPI are exact government IDs shared across datasets. Only the property match is name-based, and there we require an exact first and last name in the same state — anything less is dropped, and there are tests that exist purely to prove near-misses still get rejected.

“Why doctors?”

Because it is the cleanest public data in the country — a national registry, state licensing boards, and a federal database naming who each physician bills through. It is the easiest place to prove the idea works. Lawyers and dentists are the same pattern with a different connector.

“What would it take to run this for real?”

Three things: lift the demo cap so we pull whole specialties instead of a sample, buy the business-registry feed, and run it on a schedule so score history accumulates. None of that changes the engine.

Wording that keeps you out of trouble

Say it this way, not that way

It is a research tool, not a lead list. If someone hears “call list,” you will spend the rest of the meeting on compliance.

Ownership is inferred from Medicare billing, not read off a filing. Say “we worked it out,” never “we know.” The screen labels it as an inference — let it.

Don’t apologise for scores in the fifties and sixties. Ninety-one is the day-one ceiling, on purpose, and that is a better story than a system handing out perfect hundreds on its first run.

Beth Ann Adams is the ownership example, not a timing one. Her licence is seventeen years old. Lead the demo with Rahul Aggarwal, who actually carries the thesis.

The advisor screen on slide 8 is representative; the data behind the demo is real. Keep those two words apart — muddling them either oversells the mock or undersells the build.

If someone notices nearly every surname starts with “A”: that is a demo page limit, one parameter. Say it before they find it if you can.

These are real named people from public records. Keep the tone factual on screen — no speculating about anyone.
