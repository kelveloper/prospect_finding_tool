Slides 1 – 3

The opportunity

Slide 1Title card. Talk to the room, not the screen.

Today we’re presenting our Emerging Affluent Prospecting proof of concept.

The idea is simple. Instead of waiting until someone is already visibly wealthy and actively shopping for wealth management, can we identify potential future private-bank clients earlier, while their wealth-building is still forming?

The proof of concept looks at early, publicly available signals and turns them into prioritised and explainable prospects for advisors.

So first, let’s look at the opportunity we’re trying to address.

Slide 2Find future wealth while the need is forming.

Traditional prospecting finds people once there are already obvious indicators of wealth. We wanted to see whether we could move that discovery earlier.

For the first use case we focused on physicians. There are roughly twenty-three thousand six hundred projected physician openings a year — a population with recurring career transitions and significant future earning potential.

The engine combines public information such as NPI and licensing data with entity, property and career events. It resolves the individual, detects the signals, ranks the prospect, and tells the advisor why this person may matter now, and what they could do next.

And physicians are useful for testing this because their career contains several observable transitions.

Slide 3The hypothesis slide. This is the one a manager repeats to someone else — slow down.

The key idea is that we’re not asking “who is a successful physician?” We’re looking for change and momentum.

A physician moves from training, to licence, to entering practice, to possibly becoming an owner or partner, and eventually to larger financial milestones. Each of those transitions creates different financial needs.

A recent practice entry or ownership event is more actionable than knowing somebody has been a physician for twenty years. So the hypothesis is that recent, corroborated transitions tell us not only who may be a good prospect, but when outreach is relevant.

NoteIf you want one concrete anchor here, use the top of the board: an Illinois licence issued two months ago and a $1.1 million house bought two months ago, on the same person. That is the hypothesis, in public data, without leaving the slide.

So how do we turn those fragmented signals into something an advisor can actually use?

Slides 4 – 8

How it works

Slide 4Four live sources to one ranked prospect. Trace the flow with your cursor while you talk. Spend a little longer here — this slide is the POC.

This is the end-to-end flow. We start by fetching live public data.

Then we resolve identity — because before we combine anything, we need confidence that the records actually belong to the same person. Once identity is established, we can attach supporting evidence from the other sources.

The engine then detects the signals, scores and explains them, and turns them into an advisor-facing action. So instead of handing an advisor four disconnected datasets, the system returns one prospect with a traceable reason for why that person was prioritised. And the signals keep their source evidence the whole way through.

That identity step matters, because combining public datasets also creates the risk of attaching information to the wrong person.

Slide 5Resolve with confidence, enrich only with evidence. Emphasise the principle, not every matching rule.

We designed the matching conservatively. NPPES gives us the initial identity, and the licence number gives us a strong shared key for validating that person against Illinois licensing data.

Once that trusted identity exists, enrichment follows a stricter rule. For Medicare billing we require an exact NPI match. For Cook County property we require an exact name and state match. If the evidence doesn’t meet the rule, we drop it rather than guess.

That’s deliberate. For this use case, a missed signal is safer than attaching another person’s property, business or career to a prospect.

NotePause after that line. It should land as a design principle, not as a technical limitation. It also pre-empts the compliance question later.

It also makes the recommendation more explainable, because we know exactly what evidence was accepted and why.

Once we have a trusted prospect and trusted evidence, the next question is: what actually counts as a meaningful signal?

Slide 6Signals explain fit, or a moment to act.

We separate signals into two different questions.

The first is qualification — is this person potentially a strong future-wealth prospect? Specialty earning potential, active professional standing, and practice or entity ownership answer that.

The second is timing — is there a reason this person is particularly relevant now? A recent licence, practice entry, property event, or career advancement.

And we deliberately don’t treat any single event as proof of wealth. Buying a property on its own does not make somebody an affluent prospect. The value comes from combining corroborated signals.

And that split between qualification and timing also drives how we rank.

Slide 7Fit must be true before timing matters.

The prototype weights qualification more heavily than timing. Sixty percent of the score is future-wealth fit; forty percent is the moment to act.

Within qualification we look at active standing, specialty potential, and ownership evidence. Timing looks at licence recency, property events, practice entry, and career advancement.

The important thing is that sixty-forty isn’t necessarily the production weighting. These are transparent prototype weights. The goal of the POC is to show we can build an explainable ranking where every point traces back to a source, a date, and a matched fact. In production those weights would be calibrated against actual advisor outcomes.

NoteSay that last part. Otherwise the first question you get is “why sixty-forty?” — answer it before anyone needs to ask.

But a score on its own isn’t useful to an advisor. So the next step is turning that score into something actionable.

Slide 8The advisor sees the evidence and the next move.

This is what the advisor experience should ultimately provide. Rather than just a score, the advisor sees the prospect together with the evidence behind the recommendation.

In this example the system shows profession and location, then the events contributing to the recommendation. Then we translate those signals into two things. Why now — what changed that made this prospect relevant. And what next — what might be an appropriate reason for outreach.

So instead of telling an advisor “here’s someone with a score of eighty-two,” we give them the context to understand the recommendation and decide whether to act.

CarefulThe person on this slide is fictional and the slide says so. Don’t narrate her as though she is a real prospect — say “representative example.” The real named people come up in the demo, one slide later.

Slide 10The core works today. This slide stops anyone assuming the POC already does everything.

What we’ve shown is a working end-to-end core. Today we have four live public sources — the national physician registry, Illinois licensing, Medicare billing enrolment and Cook County property — behind source adapters and a normalised schema, then identity resolution, signal detection, scoring, and an advisor-facing experience. All four are free government APIs. Our data cost today is zero.

Moving toward production would need broader data agreements and coverage, stronger data-quality and governance controls, authentication and audit, and integration into the advisor’s existing workflow — Coach or the CRM. And collecting advisor outcomes, so the ranking can eventually be calibrated on what actually leads to meaningful engagement.

So stepping back from the implementation, the POC helps us answer four questions.

Slide 11The decision. This is a conclusion, not another architecture explanation.

We evaluated the concept across four dimensions.

Is it desirable? Physicians give us a repeatable population of career and financial transitions where earlier prospecting may create value.

Is it feasible? The proof of concept shows a working path from public-data ingestion through ranking and into an advisor-facing experience.

Is it credible? Recommendations trace back to dated public evidence, rather than arriving as an unexplained score.

Is it expandable? The architecture is built around adapters, so the same core could extend to other professions, states and data sources.

So the opportunity isn’t another list of leads. It’s an engine that helps advisors see who may be worth engaging, why they were surfaced, and why now.

Backup

NPPES is the spine

Do not presentSkip this unless somebody asks where the data actually comes from. Then bring it up, answer, and go back.

We start with the national doctor registry. That gives us who they are, their specialty, and their state license number. We take those license numbers to the Illinois licensing board, which tells us when they were licensed and whether it’s still active. We take their NPI to Medicare, which tells us what company they bill under. And we take their names to Cook County deed records.

The important bit is that each source is queried using the answer from the one before it. We never bulk-download anything. We only ever ask about people we’re already tracking.

Then three things happen. We work out which records belong to the same human. We turn the facts into signals. And we weigh the signals into a score.

DoThese two rules are the answer to most objections. Say them slowly.

Two rules we don’t bend. One: the scoring is completely deterministic — there is no AI anywhere inside it. Same data in, same number out. That’s what makes it defensible. Two: a wrong match is worse than a missed one. If we can’t prove two records are the same person, we throw it away rather than guess.

Sixty automated tests, all passing. Four government APIs, all free. Our data cost today is zero.

Cut firstDrop this if you’re over time — but keep “our data cost today is zero,” managers remember that one.

Our starting point is NPPES, which gives us the NPI, the specialty and the licence number we use to establish the physician record.

From there, Illinois licensing adds status and timing, Medicare billing adds the entity a physician is paid through, and Cook County property adds property events.

The architectural point is that NPPES goes first, and its identifiers drive targeted queries into the other three — rather than blindly combining datasets. We only ever ask about physicians we already hold.

If pressed on speedNPPES has to finish first because it supplies the keys. The other three don’t depend on each other, so they now run side by side, and the whole sweep runs in the background — about two minutes rather than four to eight blocking ones.

Questions you will get

“Is any of this a privacy problem?”

All four sources are public government datasets — the same records anyone can look up. Nothing is scraped, nothing is bought, no personal data is inferred beyond what the records say. Worth adding plainly: this is research output, not an approved outreach list. A human reviews before anyone is contacted.

“Why sixty-forty?”

Say this on slide 7 before it gets asked. They are transparent prototype weights, set so fit outranks timing — a strong prospect at an average moment still matters more than a weak one at a perfect moment. In production they would be calibrated against advisor outcomes, which is why we already store every good-fit and not-a-fit verdict.

“Why is nobody scoring above sixty-something?”

Missing inputs, not missing logic. Two things fill it in: paid business-registry data, which proves practice ownership instead of us inferring it, and career moves, which need a second monthly sync to compare against. Ninety-one is the day-one ceiling by design — anything above it has to be earned by watching someone over time.

“Where’s the AI?”

Deliberately outside the scoring. The score has to survive a compliance conversation, so it stays rules-based and deterministic — same data in, same number out. Where AI helps is around it: proposing matches on ambiguous names, where the rules still decide, and writing the plain-English summary an advisor reads.

“How do you know two records are the same person?”

Mostly we don’t have to guess. The licence number and the NPI are exact government IDs shared across datasets. Only the property match is name-based, and there we require an exact first and last name in the same state — anything less is dropped, and there are tests that exist purely to prove near-misses still get rejected.

“Why doctors?”

Because it is the cleanest public data in the country — a national registry, state licensing boards, and a federal database naming who each physician bills through. It is the easiest place to prove the idea works. Lawyers and dentists are the same pattern with a different connector.

“What would it take to run this for real?”

Three things: lift the demo cap so we pull whole specialties instead of a sample, buy the business-registry feed, and run it on a schedule so score history accumulates. None of that changes the engine.

It is a research tool, not a lead list. If someone hears “call list,” you will spend the rest of the meeting on compliance.

Don’t apologise for scores in the fifties and sixties. Ninety-one is the day-one ceiling, on purpose, and that is a better story than a system handing out perfect hundreds on its first run.
