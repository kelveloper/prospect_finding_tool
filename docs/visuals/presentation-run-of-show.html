Before the room is watching

Pre-flight

  • Start the backend on the real database. .venv/bin/uvicorn app.main:app --port 8000 — the default prospects.db holds the 219 scored physicians. Do not point DATABASE_URL at a file that does not exist; SQLite creates it empty and you will be on stage with a blank board.
  • Start the frontend — cd frontend && npm run dev, then open localhost:3000.
  • Do not run a live ingest on stage. It returns immediately and runs in the background now, but it still calls four government APIs and takes about two minutes; a second start returns 409, and the weekly gate returns 429 unless you pass force=true. Run it beforehand if you want fresh data.
  • Load the screens in tabs ahead of time so no click waits on a spinner: the opening page, the board, Rahul Aggarwal, his score breakdown, his contact kit, and the book view. That is the whole demo — you never open a second prospect.
  • Beth Ann Adams is your back pocket, not a demo step. She is the answer if someone asks about practice ownership, or challenges why an established doctor ranks highly. Have her open in a spare tab and leave her there.
  • Sanity check — curl localhost:8000/health returns ok, and pytest -q shows 76 passed. Worth having that terminal open if the manager asks.
  • Know your top name. Rahul Aggarwal is rank 1 and carries the whole thesis: Illinois license two months ago, a $1,100,000 house two months ago. If the board has been re-ingested, check who is on top before you walk in.
  • Agree the handoff line. Each speaker ends by naming the next person. It sounds rehearsed because it is.
Speaker 1

The problem, and what we built

0:00 – 1:20
Do · the problemOpen on the title slide. Talk to the room, not the screen.

Today we’re presenting our Emerging Affluent Prospecting proof of concept.

So here’s the problem. Every advisor is chasing the same people — the ones who are already rich. But by the time someone shows up on a wealth list, they’ve usually had an advisor for ten years. At that point you’re not really pitching. You’re trying to break up a marriage.

So we asked a different question. Could we find these people earlier, while they’re still building it?

NoteLand the problem before you mention doctors, data or scores. If the room doesn’t feel “too late,” nothing after this matters.
Do · why physiciansMove to the opportunity slide.

We started with doctors. There are around twenty-three thousand new openings a year, and a doctor’s career leaves a public trail — first a license, then a practice, then ownership.

Cut firstIf you are running long, drop the openings figure and start at “a career that leaves a public trail.” Nothing later depends on the number.

And we’re not really looking for status. We’re looking for change. A brand new license tells us more than twenty years of experience does. So the idea is simple: recent changes tell us two things — who’s worth calling, and when to call them.

NoteThat last sentence is the thesis. Pause after it — it is the line a manager repeats to somebody else.
Do · the solutionMove to the engine slide. This is the “so we built it” beat.

And here’s the thing that made this worth building. All of those changes are public record. They were just sitting in four separate government databases, and nobody had put them together.

So that’s what we built. It pulls all four sources, works out which records actually belong to the same person, and ranks them. And it doesn’t just hand back a number — it tells the advisor why this person matters now, and what to do next.

Two hundred and nineteen Illinois doctors, ranked. [Speaker 2] will show you what that looks like.

Speaker 3

How it works, and the decision

3:40 – 5:30
DoPut this diagram up and trace it left to right with your cursor while you talk.

Underneath, it’s all one pipeline. We start with the national doctor registry, which gives us who they are, their specialty, and their state license number. Then we take those license numbers to Illinois licensing, their NPI to Medicare, and their names to Cook County property records.

The important part is the order. The registry has to go first, because it’s what gives us the IDs for the other three. We never bulk-download anything — we only ever ask about doctors we already have.

Then three things happen. We work out which records belong to the same person, we turn those facts into signals, and we weigh the signals into a score.

DoThese two rules answer most objections. Say them slowly.

There are two rules we don’t bend. The first is that there’s no AI anywhere in the scoring. Same data in, same number out, every time. That’s what makes it defensible in a compliance conversation.

And the second is that a missed signal is safer than a wrong one. If we can’t prove two records are the same person, we drop it rather than guess. Attaching someone else’s house to a prospect would quietly break the whole thing.

NotePause after that second rule. It should sound like a design principle, not a technical limitation.

Seventy-six automated tests, all passing. Four government APIs, all free. So our data cost today is zero.

Cut firstDrop the test count if you are over — but keep “our data cost today is zero.” Managers remember that one.
DoWhat production would add. This stops anyone assuming the POC already does everything.

So what we’ve shown you is a working core, end to end. To take it to production we’d need broader data agreements, stronger data-quality and governance controls, authentication and audit, and it would need to live inside the tools advisors already use — Coach, or the CRM.

And we’d need to collect real advisor outcomes, so we can tune the ranking on what actually works instead of on weights we chose ourselves. We’re already storing every one of those answers for exactly that reason.

Do · the closeThe decision slide. This is a conclusion, not more architecture.

So stepping back, we looked at this from four angles.

Is it desirable? Doctors give us a repeatable group of career and financial changes, where getting there earlier genuinely creates value. Is it feasible? Well, we have a working path from public data, through ranking, into a screen an advisor can actually use.

Is it credible? Every recommendation traces back to dated public evidence, so it’s never just an unexplained score. And is it expandable? The whole thing is built around adapters, so the same core extends to other professions and other states. That’s one new connector each time — the scoring engine doesn’t change at all.

So the opportunity here isn’t another list of leads. It’s an engine that tells an advisor who is worth calling, why they showed up, and why now.

NoteThen stop. That is the ending — don’t add a summary after it. “Happy to take questions” is fine; anything more dilutes it.
Whoever knows it best answers — don’t all jump in

Questions you will get

“Is any of this a privacy problem?”

All four sources are public government datasets — the same records anyone can look up. Nothing is scraped, nothing is bought, no personal data is inferred beyond what the records say. Worth adding plainly: this is research output, not an approved outreach list. A human reviews before anyone is contacted.

“Why sixty-forty?”

Say this on slide 7 before it gets asked. They are transparent prototype weights, set so fit outranks timing — a strong prospect at an average moment still matters more than a weak one at a perfect moment. In production they would be calibrated against advisor outcomes, which is why we already store every good-fit and not-a-fit verdict.

“Is the book actually split per advisor?”

No — and say so straight. The advisor ID is displayed in the header, but nothing is scoped to it: there is no assignment on the prospect record and no filtering by it. We show it because a shared list is how a prospect gets called twice, and we wanted the identity carried through the UI rather than bolted on later. Who owns which prospect is a data and policy question we have not answered.

“Did you look at what already exists?”

Yes — Catchlight and Aidentified on the wealth side, Apollo and ZoomInfo on the sales side, and there is a written teardown of how each of them presents a prospect. The conventions are settled, so we used them: a saved view, a chip saying why a row is here, a confidence indicator. Nothing on screen should need training. What none of them show is the source record behind the claim, and that is where our own effort went.

“That dermatologist is nineteen years in and owns her practice — surely she already has an advisor?”

Probably, yes — and the tool agrees with you: her timing score is 5.5 out of 100. She ranks on fit, not on timing. The honest limitation underneath it is that the ownership signal comes from Medicare billing, which records who is paid today and carries no formation date, so we cannot yet tell a practice formed last month from one formed in 2006. That is a known gap with a written-up fix — the state business registry supplies formation dates. Meanwhile the top of the board is driven by timing, which is why Rahul Aggarwal is first and she is eleventh.

“Why is nobody scoring above sixty-something?”

Missing inputs, not missing logic. Two things fill it in: paid business-registry data, which proves practice ownership instead of us inferring it, and career moves, which need a second monthly sync to compare against. Ninety-one is the day-one ceiling by design — anything above it has to be earned by watching someone over time.

“Where’s the AI?”

Deliberately outside the scoring. The score has to survive a compliance conversation, so it stays rules-based and deterministic — same data in, same number out. Where AI helps is around it: proposing matches on ambiguous names, where the rules still decide, and writing the plain-English summary an advisor reads.

“How do you know two records are the same person?”

Mostly we don’t have to guess. The license number and the NPI are exact government IDs shared across datasets. Only the property match is name-based, and there we require an exact first and last name in the same state — anything less is dropped, and there are tests that exist purely to prove near-misses still get rejected.

“Why doctors?”

Because it is the cleanest public data in the country — a national registry, state licensing boards, and a federal database naming who each physician bills through. It is the easiest place to prove the idea works. Lawyers and dentists are the same pattern with a different connector.

“What would it take to run this for real?”

Three things: lift the demo cap so we pull whole specialties instead of a sample, buy the business-registry feed, and run it on a schedule so score history accumulates. None of that changes the engine.

Wording that keeps you out of trouble

Say it this way, not that way

It is a research tool, not a lead list. If someone hears “call list,” you will spend the rest of the meeting on compliance.

Ownership is inferred from Medicare billing, not read off a filing. Say “we worked it out,” never “we know.” The screen labels it as an inference — let it.

Don’t apologise for scores in the fifties and sixties. Ninety-one is the day-one ceiling, on purpose, and that is a better story than a system handing out perfect hundreds on its first run.

Beth Ann Adams is the ownership example, not a timing one. Her license is seventeen years old. Lead the demo with Rahul Aggarwal, who actually carries the thesis.

The advisor screen on slide 8 is representative; the data behind the demo is real. Keep those two words apart — muddling them either oversells the mock or undersells the build.

If someone notices nearly every surname starts with “A”: that is a demo page limit, one parameter. Say it before they find it if you can.

These are real named people from public records. Keep the tone factual on screen — no speculating about anyone.
