1 · The intro — about 90 seconds, nothing on screen

He has not seen this project, so say what it is before you say why it exists. Two short parts, and no preamble — open straight on the first line.

First — what it is

This is a proof of concept we built for prospecting at the private bank.

It pulls public government data about doctors, works out which of them an advisor should spend time on, and ranks them with the reason attached. Real data, real scores, running end to end. A prototype, not a product.

And the idea behind it is this.

Say “proof of concept” in the first sentence. It sets his expectations correctly, so when he spots something missing you are confirming what you told him rather than defending a gap. Add who else worked on it if that is useful to him.

Then — the problem it solves

Every advisor is chasing the same people. The ones who are already rich. But by the time somebody shows up on a wealth list, they have usually had an advisor for ten years. So you are not really pitching to them at that point.

So we asked a different question. Could we find people earlier, while they are still building it?

And we started with doctors, because a doctor’s career leaves a public trail. They get a license. They enter practice. Some of them open their own practice. Some of them buy a house. And every one of those is public record — they were just sitting in four separate government databases that nobody had joined up.

So we joined them up, scored everybody, and ranked them.

Stop there and share your screen. Do not explain the architecture yet.

2 · The demo — three things an advisor gets

Say this before you click anything. It gives him a shape to hang the next two minutes on, and it stops the demo feeling like a tour.

There are three things this gives an advisor. Who to call first. Everything we found out about them. And where to start the conversation. Let me show you all three on one person.

DoShare on the opening page, before pressing Begin.

This is what an advisor sees when they open it. Three things — who they are, what came in today, and a button to start.

One · who to call first

DoPress Begin. Land on the board.

Two hundred and nineteen real Illinois-licensed doctors, pulled from federal and state registries, ranked best first. So an advisor is not choosing who to work on. The list already did that.

Say “Illinois-licensed”, not “Illinois doctors”. 183 of the 219 actually practise there; the rest hold an Illinois license and work elsewhere. Small word, and it is the true one.

DoOpen Rahul Aggarwal, number one. Everything from here is him.

And this is who it put at the top today. He is a cardiologist who got his Illinois license two months ago — but his national registration is seven years old. So he is not a new graduate. He is an established doctor moving into Illinois.

And two months ago he bought a house here, for one point one million dollars. So somebody who has been earning for seven years has just relocated and just bought a home. That is a person whose financial relationships are being rebuilt right now.

The most important sentence in the demo

And here is what I would pay attention to. Somebody further down this list bought a house for more than twice as much, and he still ranks above her. Because hers was two years ago and his was two months ago.

The bigger house scores lower. That is the whole idea — this is not a rich list, it is a timing engine.

CuttableOpen his score breakdown only if he wants the arithmetic. The table below is your crib sheet.

The sixty-two is two numbers, not one. Should we care about this person — he scores fifty-six, because cardiology is a middle tier for us and he does not own a practice. And why now — he scores seventy-two, because the license and the house are both two months old. Sixty percent of the first, forty percent of the second.

So he is first not because he is the wealthiest person here. He is not. He is first because he is the one something is happening to.

Two · everything we found

DoOpen his sources page — the written summary and the evidence under it.

Then the second thing. An advisor should not have to piece this together themselves, so we write it up. This paragraph is the whole research summary — who he is, what changed, and why that matters.

And underneath it, every fact is grouped and sourced. His career signals. Whether he owns a practice. His financial activity. Each one says which government record it came from.

DoPoint at the match evidence and the confidence figure.

And this part matters more than it looks. We are joining four separate databases, so the obvious risk is that we put the wrong person’s house on somebody’s file.

So we show our working. His registry record and his license record matched on the license number, which is exact. His property record matched on an exact name in the same state. Confidence is one point zero. If we could not prove a match, we would drop it rather than guess.

This is the beat that earns trust with a technical lead. Do not rush it. Anyone can rank a list; showing why you believe the rows belong to the same human is the harder thing.

Three · where to start the conversation

DoOpen his contact section.

And the third thing is the starting point. The practice address, the phone number, and the one thing to open with — which for him is the new Illinois license, not the house.

Notice what it does not give them: the property. That tells us when to call. The advisor would never mention it.

Never say you would reference the property in the outreach. The system deliberately withholds it. Saying otherwise turns a demo into a compliance conversation.

DoPoint at the outreach buttons. Do not click through them.

And when the advisor actually reaches out, they log what happened here. Did they speak to them, did they become a client, were they not a fit. Right now that is recorded, nothing more.

Stop there. Do not volunteer the roadmap. “Recorded, nothing more” stops him assuming it feeds the score, without turning into a promise about when it will.

CuttableSwitch to the book view if there is time. Nothing later depends on it.

That was one person. Day to day an advisor works a list, so it is the same data in two shapes — the board for scanning, the book for working through. And they can filter it and save that view with a name, so Monday starts where Friday stopped.

So — who to call first, everything we know about them, and where to start. That is the tool.

Then stop.

“That is the tool. Happy to go into any part of it.”
Now be quiet and let him ask. His questions tell you which screen to open next.

3 · The questions you will get

What is the difference between the score and the evidence badge?

They answer two different questions. The score is how good this prospect looks. The evidence is how much we actually know about them.

We look for seven signals on each person. The evidence badge is just how many of the seven we found — five or more is strong, four is partial, three or fewer is thin. The score is what those signals are worth once they are weighted.

So you can have a high score built on thin evidence. Alex Brewer scores higher than Rahul on fit — seventy-five against fifty-six — but we hold fewer facts about him. That is why we show both numbers instead of blending them into one.

The three headings on the sources page — Career Signal, Ownership & Practice, Financial Activity — are only how that page is grouped for reading. They are not how the badge is calculated. Say that plainly if he asks.

Does it look up one person at a time?

No, it is a bulk sweep. One run pulls hundreds of doctors, resolves them, scores them all and ranks them — that is where the two hundred and nineteen come from. The diagram follows a single person through, but only because that is easier to read than three hundred at once.

Pre-empt this one. It is exactly what confused a reader of the diagram.

Do those outreach answers feed back into the ranking?

Not yet. Right now they are only stored. But that is the loop we would want to close — those are real outcomes we could tune the weights against, instead of the ones we picked ourselves.

Only say this if asked. Nothing in the scoring code reads these events, and he may well check. “Not yet” first, then the intent — in that order.

How is the score calculated?

Two questions. First, should we care about this person at all — that is sixty percent, and it is things like their specialty and whether they own a practice. Second, why now — that is forty percent, and it is all recency.

Those weights are ours, and they are not calibrated yet. In production we would tune them against what advisors actually convert.

Can I filter to just the people who bought a house?

Yes, in the book view — you can filter and save the view. There are no pre-built default filters yet, which is a fair thing to add.

Do not oversell it. Filter, save, name — that is what exists.

Is the book split per advisor?

No. The advisor ID is shown, but nothing is scoped to it yet. Who owns which prospect is a data question we have not answered.

Never describe it as built.

That dermatologist is nineteen years in — would she not already have an advisor?

Probably, yes. And the tool agrees — her timing score is five and a half out of a hundred. She ranks on fit, not on timing.

The honest gap is that our ownership signal comes from Medicare billing, which says who is paid today but carries no start date. So we cannot yet tell a practice formed last month from one formed in 2006. It is written up, and the fix is the state business registry.

4 · Only if he asks how it is built

Show this first, not the full diagram. Four boxes, about twenty seconds. You can draw it on a whiteboard from memory. If he wants more, Five Levels of the Same Diagram steps up one idea at a time — stop wherever he stops nodding.

Four public sources go in. First we work out which records are the same human — that is the hard part, and we drop anything we cannot prove. Then we look at what changed for that person and how recently. And out comes a ranked list, with the reason attached to each name.

Only if he wants more than that — then open the full diagram.

Open One Doctor, End to End and use the “Saying it out loud” section at the bottom. Two things to say first, because both confused a reader:

  • “Row” means a record, not a person. Row 1 is her registry record, row 2 her license record. Five facts about one doctor — not five doctors.
  • This follows one person, but a real run does hundreds at once.

If he starts giving architecture feedback, that is a good sign. Write it down rather than defending it. You do not have to have every answer in a one-to-one.
