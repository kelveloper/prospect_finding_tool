1 · The intro — about 60 seconds, nothing on screen

He has not seen this project. Open straight on the first line — no preamble — and frame it as an opportunity, not a complaint about how advisors work today.

We built a proof of concept for prospecting at the private bank.

It pulls public government data about doctors, works out which of them an advisor should spend time on, and ranks them with the reason attached. It is a working prototype — but it is real data and real scores, running end to end.

So the opportunity is to find prospects earlier than anyone else. Before they are wealthy, and before they already have an advisor.

And we started with doctors, because their careers leave a public trail. They get a license. They enter practice. Some of them open their own practice. Some of them buy a house. And every one of those is public record.

So we joined those separate government databases together, scored everybody, and ranked them.

Say “proof of concept” in the first sentence. It sets his expectations, so when he spots something missing you are confirming what you told him rather than defending a gap. Add who else worked on it if that helps.

Stop there and share your screen. Do not explain the architecture yet.

3 · The questions you will get

What is the difference between the score and the evidence badge?

They answer two different questions. The score is how good this prospect looks. The evidence is how much we actually know about them.

We look for seven signals on each person. The evidence badge is just how many of the seven we found — five or more is strong, four is partial, three or fewer is thin. The score is what those signals are worth once they are weighted.

Then the example that makes it land:

So you can have a high score built on thin evidence. Ameen Barghi scores higher than Mohamad on fit — sixty-two point four against fifty-seven point one — but we hold fewer facts about him: four of the seven signals against Mohamad’s five. The one at the top of the board is the one we know least about. That is why we show both numbers instead of blending them into one.

The three headings on the sources page — Career Signal, Ownership & Practice, Financial Activity — are only how that page is grouped for reading. They are not how the badge is calculated. Say that plainly if he asks.

Does it look up one person at a time?

No, it is a bulk sweep. One run pulls hundreds of doctors, resolves them, scores them all and ranks them — that is where the two hundred and seventy-six come from. The diagram follows a single person through, but only because that is easier to read than three hundred at once.

Pre-empt this one. It is exactly what confused a reader of the diagram.

Do those outreach answers feed back into the ranking?

Not yet. Right now they are only stored. But that is the loop we would want to close — those are real outcomes we could tune the weights against, instead of the ones we picked ourselves.

Only say this if asked. Nothing in the scoring code reads these events, and he may well check. “Not yet” first, then the intent — in that order.

How is the score calculated?

Two questions. First, should we care about this person at all — that is sixty percent, and it is things like their specialty and whether they own a practice. Second, why now — that is forty percent, and it is all recency.

Those weights are ours, and they are not calibrated yet. In production we would tune them against what advisors actually convert.

Can I filter to just the people who bought a house?

Yes, in the book view — you can filter and save the view. There are no pre-built default filters yet, which is a fair thing to add.

Do not oversell it. Filter, save, name — that is what exists.

Is the book split per advisor?

No. The advisor ID is shown, but nothing is scoped to it yet. Who owns which prospect is a data question we have not answered.

Never describe it as built.

That dermatologist is nineteen years in — would she not already have an advisor?

Probably, yes. And the tool agrees — her timing score is five and a half out of a hundred. She ranks on fit, not on timing.

The honest gap is that our ownership signal comes from Medicare billing, which says who is paid today but carries no start date. So we cannot yet tell a practice formed last month from one formed in 2006. It is written up, and the fix is the state business registry.

When you do not know the answer

Every one of these is a better answer than a guess. An engineer will remember a wrong number long after he has forgotten a confident delivery.

THE MOVE THAT WORKS EVERY TIME

Say what you do know, then name the edge of it.

“What I can tell you is that recency drives most of the timing score. Where exactly the cut-offs sit, I would want to check rather than guess.”

IF YOU NEVER KNEW IT

“I do not know — that is Kelvin’s part. Let me get you a proper answer rather than a guess.”

“Good question, and I would be making it up. Can I come back to you on it?”

Never leave it at “I do not know.” Always add what you will do about it. That turns a gap into a next step.

IF YOU KNEW IT AND IT HAS GONE

“I have that written down — let me not misquote it at you.”

“It is on the screen actually, let me pull it up.”

Do not grope for it out loud. Reading a number off the screen looks careful; half-remembering one out loud looks shaky, even when you get it right.

IF YOU KNOW IT BUT CANNOT PUT IT INTO WORDS

“Easier if I show you — give me one second.”

“The short version is that it is two questions, sixty and forty. The full version I would rather walk you through than describe.”

Drop one level of detail, do not reach for more. There is always a simpler sentence that is still true — and showing beats explaining every time.

THE ONE PEOPLE MISS — IT IS NOT A GAP, IT IS UNDECIDED

“We have not decided that yet — it is an open question, and honestly I would want an advisor’s input on it.”

A lot of what he might ask is genuinely undecided rather than unknown: the weights, whether the book is split by advisor, how ownership gets dated. Saying “we have not decided” is a completely different answer from “I do not know”, and on a proof of concept it is the correct one. Know which you are giving.

TWO THINGS THAT BUY YOU TIME

“What is making you ask? I want to answer the right thing.”

“Let me write that down.” — then actually write it.

The first often reveals he is asking something simpler than you feared. The second shows the question landed, and gives you a reason to follow up afterwards.

Do not do these. Do not guess at a number. Do not apologise for not being technical — it discounts everything you just showed him. And do not fill the silence with related facts he did not ask for; waffle reads worse than a clean “I will check.”

5 · Study sheet — questions from the room

Not lines to read out. These are for you, beforehand — so that when one lands you are recalling something rather than composing it. Read the question, answer it out loud in your own words, then open it.

The first four a reviewer called certainties, and rated above the demo itself.

First: “how do you validate this?”

The word means six different things in this project, and they do not have the same answer. Two of them you have done well. Three of them you have not done at all. Answering the wrong one is the real risk — describe your identity matching to someone who asked whether the ranking predicts anything, and a genuine strength sounds like you are dodging.

THE MOVE — NAME TWO, LET THEM PICK

“Validate could mean two things here — whether we have got the right person, or whether the ranking actually predicts who becomes a client. Which one are you asking?”

Five seconds, and it shows you know the difference rather than stalling. Do not just ask “what do you mean?” — naming the options is what makes it read as rigour instead of evasion. If they wave you on, take the identity one first: it is the strongest and it buys you the floor.

That splits the two honestly, claims the part you earned, and hands them the roadmap in the same breath.

Proving it works

How will you evaluate whether this actually works?

“The ranking is a hypothesis — that someone we rank high converts more often than someone we rank low. Everything the tool logs is built to test it.”

Lead with this framing, not with a metric. It tells them you know the ranking is a claim and not a fact, which is the thing they are actually checking.

What would you measure?

“Conversion rate by score band. If the sixty-to-eighty band converts better than the twenty-to-forty band, the ranking is earning its place. If the line comes out flat, our weights are wrong and we retune them.”

One number, and it can be proved wrong — which is what makes it a real answer. Say it before anything about clicks or hours saved; those are easier to measure and worth much less.

What data are you collecting to support that?

“We already collect it. Every outcome an advisor logs is stored against that prospect’s score — whether we reached them, whether they became a client, and when an advisor rules someone out, the reason from a fixed list. The funnel is already broken out by score band.”

True today, so say it with confidence. The measuring instrument was built before the thing it measures. The rule-out reasons are the faster signal of the two — they say what the model is getting wrong before a single call is spent finding out. If half come back “wrong specialty”, the specialty weights are off.

What is the business value? Can you put a number on it?

“The value is reaching someone before they are obviously wealthy — because once they are obvious, somebody else has already called. I can’t put a figure on it yet. What I would measure is the time from a trigger event to first contact, and conversion by band.”

Do not invent a number. Not a percentage, not a pound figure, not “saves two hours a week”. Naming the metric you would use is the stronger answer, and a made-up number is the one thing here that can actually lose you the room.

What would you do before an advisor ever touches it?

“A task, not a demo. Sit someone in front of it and ask one question — who would you call first, and why — and see whether they can answer inside a minute with no help from me. If the ranking is not legible, nothing downstream matters.”

Small enough to actually run with a teammate before the next presentation. Running it once, even with two people, turns this from a plan into evidence.

Why this, at all

Why doctors?

“Because their careers leave a public trail. They get a licence, they enter practice, some open their own practice, some buy a house — and every one of those is a public record. Very few professions are documented that well.”

Then the honest second half: it is a test cohort, chosen because the data exists — not a claim that doctors are the best prospects in the bank. If it works here, the same shape works anywhere the trail is public. Lawyers next.

Why only Illinois?

“To prove the idea out we narrowed to one state where the licensing data is public and usable. The national pieces — the doctor registry and the Medicare billing — already cover everywhere. It is the state licence board and the property deeds that are local.”

Be exact about what scaling costs, because someone technical will ask. Another state needs a new licence adapter and a new deeds source. The other two sources come for free.

Where do the seventeen years and the million come from?

“Medscape’s physician wealth reporting. It is one source and we footnote it — if someone has better data on when physicians actually accumulate, that changes our curve and we would want it.”

Two people have now asked where these came from. Have the source ready and do not defend the exact figures — the shape of the curve is the argument, not the numbers on it.

A million is not a private bank threshold. Why that number?

“It isn’t the threshold, and we are not claiming it is. It is the marker of someone crossing from paying down debt into accumulating. The whole point is to reach them on the way up, before the threshold — not at it.”

Christina raised this directly. If the deck still says “a million” without that framing, it reads as though you think a million buys you a private bank client.

Data, trust and limits

Is this real data, or a mock-up?

“Real, and live. Four public government sources — the national doctor registry, Illinois licensing, Medicare billing records, and Cook County property deeds. These are real people; you can look any of them up.”

This lands harder than anything else in the demo. Say it early rather than waiting to be asked.

Are we allowed to use this? What about privacy?

“Every source is a public government record — nothing purchased, nothing private, nothing scraped from behind a login. And we deliberately do not surface home addresses even where the deed gives us one; the advisor gets the practice address and the practice phone.”

Volunteer the home address decision rather than waiting to be asked. It shows the restraint was deliberate, which is the thing a compliance-minded room is listening for.

Do you know how much money these people actually have?

“No, and we don’t claim to. We infer likelihood from specialty, how long they have been practising, whether they appear to own their practice, and whether they have bought property. It is a ranked guess with the reasoning attached, not a net worth figure.”

Answer the no first and plainly. Anyone who thinks you are claiming net worth will stop trusting the rest.

What if you have matched the wrong person?

“That is the risk we spent the most time on. We join four databases, so the obvious failure is putting one person’s records on someone else’s file. We only merge on evidence we can show, we drop what we cannot prove, and every profile carries the match confidence and what it was matched on.”

Then open the identity panel if it is to hand. A named, visible failure mode reads as engineering maturity — hiding it does the opposite.

Are any of these already J.P. Morgan clients?

“We don’t know yet — the tool has no view of the existing client book. That is the first integration I would want, because right now an advisor could be handed someone they already bank.”

A gap, and a good one to volunteer. A reviewer misread the “became a client” button as meaning this person is a client, so the confusion is live. Naming it before they find it is worth more than hoping they don’t.

Do all the high-earning specialties bill Medicare?

“No — and that is a real gap we know about. Cosmetic dermatology and a lot of plastic surgery run private-pay on purpose, so no Medicare record is not evidence of no practice. For those specialties the absence should not count against them, and right now it can.”

Christina raised exactly this. Do not discover it live — say it as a known limitation with a fix in mind, and it becomes a point in your favour.

Scope and after

What would it take to run this for the whole bank?

“The method carries; the plumbing is per-source. Another state needs its licence board and its property records. Another profession needs whatever its public trail is. None of that is research any more — it is adapters.”

Say what is done and what is wiring. That distinction is what tells a senior listener whether this is an idea or a prototype.

What happens to this after the apprenticeship?

“It is a proof of concept, and it is documented as one — the known gaps are written down, not buried. What we would hand over is the method and the evidence loop, so whoever picks it up starts from what we learned rather than from the idea.”

Have an answer ready. It is often the last question, it is about you rather than the project, and a shrug undoes a good hour.

Undecided is not the same as unknown. Whether the book is split per advisor, what the weights should be, whether outcomes feed back into the score — nobody has decided those yet. “We have not decided” is the correct answer on a proof of concept and a completely different answer from “I do not know”. Know which one you are giving.
